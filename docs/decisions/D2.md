## Decisions (user, 2026-11-23)

The answers to section 6's open questions:

1. **`d` for `K_d`:** a default of 5, overridable per Scatter. `d` must be
   odd. Scatters high in the stack (trees) may drop to 3 if the spike shows
   their margins cost too much.
2. **Local edits:** accepted. A local mask change can change which copies
   survive up to `d × R` outside it.
3. **Integer placement:** accepted. 32-bit hashes, fixed-point positions
   (1/256 m, relative to the chunk) and 8-bit masks, read without hardware
   filtering. Every tree moves from where it is today, and no compatibility
   is kept.
4. **Mask resolutions:** start at 0.5 m for footprints and 2 m for
   influence, 8 bits. **Not settled:** the spike and the first Ecotope must
   check these values, including whether footprint masks should follow each
   Scatter's lattice cell instead.
5. **No GPU readback for queries:** accepted. Queries place the chunk again
   on the CPU. Grass falls back to density-only queries if that is too slow.
6. **One placement contract for every Scatter** (World-anchored cells, ids
   from cells, fixed ranks, the density threshold, masks in and out). The
   lattice is the candidate source for every Scatter with spacing. Plant's
   progressive tile stays as grass's candidate source only, because its
   prefixes stay evenly spread as density drops, where a lattice thins into
   clumps. Its cells must become World cells.
7. **Margin cap:** an Ecotope warns (it does not reject, until the spike
   gives real costs) when a stack's widest window goes past one ring of
   chunks. Canopy shade comes from the tree Scatter's density field, not
   from its copies. Only hard footprints (trunks, boulders, logs) come from
   copies.
8. **Spike budgets:** 2 ms per chunk for the whole stack on an integrated
   GPU, 0.5 ms for `K_5`, and 5 ms for the CPU twin.

Written 2026-11-23 from a read of the tree, plant, stone and deadwood code,
the prior art below, and two throwaway CPU prototypes in [d2/](d2/) (their
output is checked in next to them). No GPU spike has run yet: section 5
says what it must prove.

## Bottom line

1. **One placement model for every Scatter: candidates on a World-anchored
   lattice, each with a fixed rank, kept while its rank is below the local
   density.** Tree's lattice is the model. Plant's blue-noise tile becomes
   one way to generate candidates for dense Scatters with no spacing rule,
   under the same contract, once its cells are World-anchored.
2. **Spacing becomes a bounded rule, `K_d`.** Today's grove keeps sites
   greedily in rank order, so a site's fate can hang on a chain of sites of
   any length, across chunk borders. `K_d` runs `d` rounds of "keep a site
   if no lower-ranked conflicting site was kept in the previous round". For
   odd `d` it never breaks spacing, keeps a subset of what the greedy rule
   keeps, depends only on candidates within `d × max spacing`, and keeps
   "a lower global density only removes trees". With `d = 5`, the
   prototype matches today's greedy result at all but 4 of 62,000 trees in
   the densest mix tried. With `d = 7` it matches at every tree. Recommended
   default: **`d = 5`**.
3. **Masks are per-chunk textures on the shared chunk grid, in 8-bit
   unsigned integers, read without hardware filtering.** Each Scatter
   writes its own mask layer for a chunk plus a margin. The next Scatter
   reads the product of the layers above it. Integer texels and integer
   maths keep the CPU and the GPU bit-identical.
4. **The CPU never reads GPU placement back for queries. It places the
   chunk again itself.** Placement is cheap and pure, and the CPU path is
   the reference (rule 1), so a query, a collider or a promotion runs the
   CPU twin for that chunk. Readback is for tests only. Unreal and Horizon
   both pay a readback cost for this; procgena doesn't need to.
5. **CPU/GPU parity needs integer arithmetic throughout**: 32-bit hashes
   (plant's PCG hash, not tree's 64-bit SplitMix), integer ranks, ratios
   compared by cross-multiplying, and positions in fixed point (1/256 m)
   relative to the chunk. The spike must prove it bit for bit.
6. **Reach has three parts**: spacing dependency (`d × R`), mask footprint
   (`F`, the largest radius a copy clears, giants included) and copy
   displacement (`J`, lattice jitter plus pulls such as plant's tufts).
   Margins stack down the Ecotope. Section 4 gives the formula and a cap.
7. **Slot choice (for D3)**: an integer-only, 32-bit jump hash and
   rendezvous hashing are equally balanced and equally minimal in
   moves on the CPU (prototype below). Both can run in WGSL. The spike only
   has to measure their cost on the GPU and confirm parity.

Papers:

- **Wang tiles** ([Cohen et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1145/882262.882265)) and
  **recursive Wang tiles** ([Kopf et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1145/1141911.1141916)).
  Precomputed Poisson tiles whose edges match, so blue noise continues
  across tile borders. Kopf orders each tile's points progressively, so
  density is a threshold on rank, which is the same idea as Horizon's dither
  and plant's tile prefix. Plant's tile has no edge matching. It shifts each
  cell's copy of one toroidal tile by a random offset, so the blue-noise
  property breaks at every cell border.
- **Procedural object distribution** ([Lagae and Dutré 2005](https://doi.org/10.1145/1095878.1095888)):
  Poisson-disk tiles with corner constraints, evaluated locally. The closest
  published match to chunk-local, deterministic placement. Survey:
  [Lagae and Dutré 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2007.01100.x).
- **Parallel Poisson disk** ([Wei 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1360612.1360619)):
  a grid of cells r/√2 across, processed in phase groups far enough apart
  that darts can't conflict. Its result depends on the phase order, which
  breaks density nesting (a lower density no longer keeps a subset).
- **Bridson 2007** ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1145/1278780.1278807)),
  **Mitchell's best candidate** ([1991](https://doi.org/10.1145/122718.122736)),
  **maximal Poisson-disk sampling** ([Ebeida et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/2010324.1964944)):
  good quality, but sequential or global, so not chunk-local without a
  halo. Plant builds its tile with best candidate, offline.
- **Matérn hard-core process type II** (Matérn, *Spatial Variation*,
  [1986](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4615-7892-5)): drop a point if a
  lower-ranked point lies within r. One pass, strictly local, but not
  maximal. It is `K_1` below.
- **Local computation of the greedy maximal independent set** under random
  ranks ([Nguyen and Onak 2008](https://doi.org/10.1109/FOCS.2008.81),
  [Yoshida et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1536414.1536447),
  [Rubinfeld et al. 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.1377)): "is this point
  in the greedy set?" can be answered by recursing only into lower-ranked
  neighbours, at small expected cost. That is today's grove rule, and it
  explains why it is *almost* local. It is not *bounded*, though. `K_d`
  makes it bounded.

What this means for procgena:

- Nobody reads neighbouring results to place a chunk. Horizon recomputes
  the density of the layers it depends on, and Unreal forbids reading
  neighbours. Both match "a chunk is a pure function of position".
- Thresholding a fixed per-point rank against density (Horizon's dither,
  Kopf's progressive tiles, plant's tile prefix, tree's rank) is the shared
  idea. It is what gives "lower density only removes copies".
- Exclusion between layers goes through density, by subtraction or
  multiplication (Unreal's `Difference`, Horizon's dependent density maps,
  Frostbite's shader-written masks). Hard exclusion of footprints is either
  done by sharing one pattern (Horizon) or by pruning against points
  (Unreal's `Self Pruning`).
- Unreal's GPU path shows the cost of not having a CPU twin: no collision,
  no navigation. Procgena's rule 1 (a CPU reference path) avoids that.

## 2. Masks on the GPU (open question 1)

### Representation

- **One texture per Scatter per chunk**, on the shared chunk grid of
  "Loading by chunk" (64 m at full detail). Each covers the chunk plus that
  Scatter's mask apron (section 4), so a reader never needs a neighbour's
  texture.
- **Format `R8Uint`** (or `R8Unorm` read through `textureLoad`, never
  through a sampler). 0 means "fully masked", 255 "not masked". An integer
  format means the CPU and the GPU see the same texel values, and nobody
  depends on a GPU's filtering precision. Horizon and Frostbite also store
  masks in 8 bits.
- **Resolution per mask kind, set by the Scatter that writes it:**
  - *Footprint masks* (a trunk, a boulder, a log: hard exclusion) at
    **0.5 m**, the grove's lattice cell: 128 × 128 texels plus the apron,
    about 16–30 KB per chunk.
  - *Influence masks* (canopy shade thinning grass, a density-field mask
    from a dense Scatter) at **2 m**: 32 × 32 plus the apron, about 2 KB.
  - The texel grid of each mask kind is **fixed to the World, at one
    resolution in metres per Scatter, at every chunk level**. A coarser
    chunk holds more texels, never coarser ones, because a different
    resolution would change which copies survive with distance ("Placement
    does not depend on distance"). The same holds for the quantised slope
    and the Ecotope coverage texels that placement reads.
  - Who makes the quantised inputs: coverage and slope texels are computed
    once per chunk on the CPU from `Ground` and the Biome's rules, and
    uploaded, so both sides read the same bytes. Masks written by Scatters
    are computed on either side by the same integer code.
- **Reading a mask** is a fixed-point bilinear blend written by hand: four
  `textureLoad`s and integer weights in 1/256ths, the same code on the CPU
  and in WGSL. The result is an integer from 0 to 255.

### How one Scatter writes a mask and the next reads it

```text
for scatter k in the Ecotope, highest priority first:
    available_k = coverage × product(mask_j for j < k)     // texel by texel
    copies_k    = place(k, chunk, available_k, ground)     // section 3
    mask_k      = 255 everywhere in the chunk + apron
    for copy in copies_k:                                   // footprint mask
        stamp a disc of radius footprint(copy) at 0 (or a soft falloff)
    or, for a density-field Scatter:
        mask_k  = 255 − shade(density field)                // influence mask
```

- **The product is done once per chunk per Scatter**, into an
  "available" texture the next Scatter reads. It is an integer multiply
  (`a × b / 255`, rounded down), so the result is the same everywhere.
- **On the GPU**, stamping is one compute pass over the copies, writing
  with `atomicMin` into a `u32` storage buffer (WGSL has atomics only in
  storage buffers and workgroup memory), then a pass that packs it to an
  8-bit texture. Each
  stamp only lowers values, so the order of writes doesn't matter.
- **On the CPU**, the same stamping runs over the same copies.
- Horizon's "layered dithering" appears, on our unverified reading of its
  slides, to let layers with the same footprint share one pattern and take
  disjoint threshold bands. A grove already mixes species much like that
  (`pick_tree`). Whether it is worth using between Scatters with equal
  footprints is left open until the talk is checked.

### How CPU queries see GPU placement

They don't read it back. A query (a ray cast, `within`, a collider, a
promotion) calls the CPU twin of `place` for the chunks it touches. The
result is bit-identical, because both sides use integer maths (section 3).
That also gives a server with no GPU the same world (story 8). Readback
stays as a test tool: the parity tests compare the two.

The cost is CPU time per queried chunk. The spike measures it (section 5).
If it is too slow for some Scatter (grass at hundreds per m²), that
Scatter answers density queries from its mask, as plant does today
("density sample only" in the alignment table), and doesn't support
per-copy queries.

## 3. Placement algorithm

### Recommendation: tree's lattice, generalised

Every Scatter places the same way:

1. **Candidates on a World-anchored lattice.** One candidate per cell of
   side `c` (0.5 m for trees). Its position is the cell plus a hashed
   jitter. Its id is the World cell index. Its rank is a hash. A Scatter
   denser than one copy per cell uses a finer `c`. A dense Scatter with no
   spacing rule (grass) may instead use plant's progressive tile as its
   candidate source, as long as the tile's cells are World cells and its
   rank is the tile index.
2. **Density threshold.** Keep a candidate while
   `rank / local < candidates_per_cell`, where `local` is the
   available mask times the Ecotope's coverage. In integers:
   `u24 × 255 < p × local_u8 × 2^24`, with `p` held as a fixed-point
   integer, so every comparison is exact.
3. **Ground and slope** (`stand`, the footing check). These need `Ground`
   over the candidate's footprint.
4. **Spacing** with `K_d` (section 4), only for Scatters that have a
   spacing.
5. **Mask out** (section 2).

Why the lattice rather than plant's tiles:

- **Copy ids come free.** A lattice cell is a stable copy id (rule 4). A
  tile point's id is "cell plus point index", which also works, but only
  once the cell is World-anchored. Today plant's cell hash uses the
  chunk's ids (`chunk.ids` in `generate.wesl`), so the whole pattern moves
  with the field.
- **Spacing between different species needs neighbour lookups.** On a
  lattice of one candidate per cell, a neighbour is a fixed stencil of
  cells, so `K_d` is a plain stencil pass on the GPU. A shifted tile has no
  structure across cells.
- **Blue noise from the tile breaks at every cell border** (the random
  offset per cell), so the tile's main advantage only holds inside a cell.
- **Determinism across chunk build order** is the same for both, once both
  are World-anchored: each candidate is a pure function of its cell.

Plant's tile stays worth keeping for grass: it gives an even spread with no
spacing pass at hundreds of blades per m², and its prefix is already the
rank. Under the contract above it is a candidate source, not a second
placement model.

### CPU/GPU parity

Rule 5 asks for the same copies on the CPU and the GPU. Floats make that
fragile: WGSL promises correctly rounded `+`, `−` and `×`, but compilers
may fuse them into FMA, and division may be off by 2.5 ULP. So:

| Quantity | Today (tree) | For parity |
|----------|--------------|------------|
| Hash | SplitMix64 (`mix`), 64-bit | A 32-bit hash (plant's PCG `hash`/`hash2`), WGSL has no 64-bit integers |
| Cell index | `i32` from `f32` positions | `i32` World cell index; CPU works it out from `f64`, GPU gets the chunk's first cell as an integer |
| Rank | `f32` draw divided by the mask | `u24` draw, compared by cross-multiplying with the 8-bit mask |
| Position | `f32` metres | Fixed point, 1/256 m, relative to the chunk origin (`i32`). Converted to `f32` only for the transform. Squared distances fit in `u32` up to 128 m apart |
| Spacing test | `f32` squared distance | Integer squared distance against an integer squared spacing |
| Mask | `f32` closure | 8-bit texels, fixed-point bilinear |
| Ground height and slope | `f32` from `Ground` | Not bit-exact across CPU and GPU. It decides the footing check and the height. Proposal: the *keep* decision for slope uses a quantised slope (a byte per texel, like a mask), so it is exact; the height itself may differ by an ULP, which nothing keys on |

### Determinism across build order

With the lattice, a candidate is a pure function of `(World seed, Scatter
id, cell)`, and `K_d` is a pure function of the candidates within
`d × R`. So a chunk computed from candidates over its padded window gives
exactly the copies the whole World would. The prototype checks this:
chunks of 16 m and 64 m, each built alone with a margin of `d × R`, give
0 differences against the whole area in every case.

## 4. Chunk reach (open question 6)

### Why today's grove spacing is not chunk-local

`GrovePlacement::new` sorts the kept candidates by rank and keeps one if no
already-kept tree lies within the larger of the two spacings. That is the
greedy maximal independent set in rank order. Its result is a pure
function of the ranks, but site *A* depends on *B* (lower rank, close
by), which depends on *C*, and so on. The chain has no fixed length. A
chunk built with a finite margin can disagree with the whole World. In the
prototype, a margin of 1 × spacing gave 1 or 2 wrong trees per 128 m
square in the denser cases, and 2 × spacing gave none. That is
probabilistic, not a guarantee. Deadwood's `StickScatter` is worse: it
keeps sticks in grid-scan order, so its result depends on where the scan
starts.

### The redesign: `K_d`, bounded rank spacing

```text
// Candidates: World-anchored lattice cells, already through the density
// threshold and the footing check. Each has an id, a fixed-point position,
// a spacing s, and an integer rank r (ties broken by id).
// conflict(a, b) := |pos_a − pos_b|² < max(s_a, s_b)²
// lower(a)       := { b : conflict(a, b) and (r_b, id_b) < (r_a, id_a) }

fn place_chunk(chunk, d):                       // d odd, default 5
    R = max spacing of the Scatter
    window = chunk grown by d × R               // section "Margins" adds J
    C = candidates(window)                      // pure per cell
    kept_prev = { c: true for c in C }          // K_0: everyone
    repeat d times:                             // K_1 .. K_d
        kept = { c: not any(kept_prev[b] for b in lower(c)) for c in C }
        kept_prev = kept
    return [c for c in C if kept[c] and owner(c) == chunk]
```

Why it is order-independent and safe:

- **Bounded.** `K_n` at a candidate reads `K_{n−1}` only of candidates
  within `R`. So `K_d` reads candidates within `d × R`, and nothing else.
  A chunk with that margin gives exactly the whole World's answer, in any
  build order, with any chunk size.
- **Never breaks spacing (odd `d`).** The step
  `F(S) = { c : no lower-ranked conflicting b in S }` reverses inclusion,
  and the greedy set `G` is its fixed point. Starting from everyone,
  `K_1 ⊆ K_3 ⊆ … ⊆ G ⊆ … ⊆ K_4 ⊆ K_2`. If two conflicting sites `a`
  (lower rank) and `b` were both in `K_{2m+1}`, then `a ∉ K_{2m}`, but
  `K_{2m+1} ⊆ G ⊆ K_{2m}`: a contradiction. Even `d` can break spacing.
- **Nested in global density.** A candidate's `K_d` depends only on
  candidates of lower rank. Lowering the global scale removes the
  highest-ranked candidates, so every surviving decision is unchanged:
  "a lower density only removes trees" still holds, as today. For a
  *local* mask change it holds only where the mask changed: a change
  reranks candidates there, and can change survivors up to `d × R` beyond
  it. (Today's rule has the same effect with no bound.) In the prototype,
  clearing or raising the mask in a 6 m disc changed no site outside it,
  for the greedy rule either. That is one disc at one spot, a single
  sample, not evidence that the bound is loose in general.
- **Close to today's result.** `K_1` is Matérn II: strictly local, but in
  the densest mix it keeps only 76% of the greedy result. `K_3` keeps 99.4%,
  `K_5` all but 4 of 62,000, `K_7` all of them.

Prototype results ([d2/spacing_prototype.py](d2/spacing_prototype.py),
[output](d2/spacing_prototype.out)), 128 m square under an uneven mask:

| Case | Greedy keeps | `K_1` | `K_3` | `K_5` | `K_7` |
|------|-------------:|------:|------:|------:|------:|
| Pine large, scale 1 (1.3 m) | 493 | 0.998 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Oak large, scale 3 (2.7 m) | 299 | 0.970 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Ash large + aspen small, scale 2 (2.5 / 0.6 m) | 4080 | 0.883 | 0.9995 | 1 | 1 |
| Ash large + aspen small, scale 4 | 7155 | 0.759 | 0.994 | 0.9999 | 1 |
| Pine small, scale 4, saturated (0.6 m) | 7458 | 0.972 | 0.9999 | 1 | 1 |
| Same dense mix, 384 m square: sites that differ from greedy | 62,024 kept | 15,403 | 310 | 4 | 0 |

All `K_d` (odd) results had zero spacing violations and were subsets of the
greedy result.

**On the GPU**, `K_d` is `d` ping-pong passes over the padded lattice. With
one candidate per 0.5 m cell, the neighbours of a site are a fixed stencil:
±⌈R / c⌉ + 1 cells, so ±7 for oak's 2.7 m (a 15 × 15 window, 225 reads).
A 64 m chunk padded by 5 × 2.7 m is about 182 × 182 cells, so five rounds
read about 37 million texels if every cell is visited. That is roughly a
millisecond on a discrete GPU, and more on an integrated one (an estimate;
the spike measures it). The 0.5 ms budget in section 5 assumes a stream
compaction of the survivors of the density threshold first, which cuts the
work by the keep ratio (about 0.28 candidates per cell in the densest case),
and early exit on empty cells.

### Choosing each Scatter's reach and margin

Three separate distances, each stated by the Scatter:

- **`S` (spacing dependency)** = `d × R`, where `R` is the largest spacing
  in the Scatter (the larger of two species applies).
- **`J` (displacement)**: how far a copy can stand from its lattice cell
  corner: one cell of jitter, plus any pull (plant's tufts move a root up
  to 0.95 of a tuft's size). It decides chunk ownership and how far a
  copy reaches out of its chunk.
- **`F` (mask footprint)**: the largest radius a copy clears or shades in
  its mask. For trees that is the trunk or canopy radius times the largest
  scale `tree_scale` can return (giants included). Canopy shade is an
  influence mask at 2 m, so `F` for shade is the canopy radius.

`ARCHITECTURE.md`'s "maximum reach across chunk borders" is `J + F`: how far
outside its chunk a copy can stand or reach. The margin a chunk must be
*placed* over is larger, and it stacks down the Ecotope.

### Margins stack down the Ecotope

To place Scatter `k` over a region, you need:

- its candidates over the region grown by `S_k + J_k`,
- and the "available" mask (the masks of every Scatter above `k`) over that.

The mask of Scatter `j` over a region needs `j`'s copies over that region
grown by `F_j`. So, from the bottom up:

```text
// need[k]: how far past the drawn chunk Scatter k's copies must be known.
// The drawn chunk itself needs every Scatter's copies over the chunk: 0.
need[k] = 0 for every k
for k from lowest priority up to highest:
    reads = need[k] + S_k + J_k          // where k reads the masks above it
    for j above k:
        need[j] = max(need[j], reads + F_j)
// Scatter j is placed over the chunk grown by need[j] + S_j + J_j.
```

Example (the forest-floor order from ARCHITECTURE.md, `d = 5`, guessed
sizes): grass (`S = 0`, `J = 0.5`), logs (`S = 5 × 1`, `F = 3`), boulders
(`S = 5 × 1.5`, `F = 2`), trees (`S = 5 × 2.7`, `F = 1` trunk, canopy shade
`F = 6`), every Scatter with `J = 0.5`. Working up the stack: logs' copies
are needed 3.5 m out, boulders' 11 m, trees' 25 m. Trees' candidates are
then needed about 39 m past the chunk: for a 64 m chunk, the trees' window
is about 2.2 × 2.2 chunks. That is the price of chaining masks through placement.
Three ways to keep it down, which can be combined:

1. **Cap the total.** The Ecotope rejects (or warns about) a stack whose
   largest window is more than one chunk ring (64 m at full detail), so a
   chunk only ever needs its 8 neighbours' ground and coverage.
2. **Density-field masks for long reaches.** Canopy shade comes from the
   tree Scatter's density field, not from its copies (as "Loading by chunk"
   already allows), so its `F` doesn't add a placement margin. Only hard
   footprints (trunks, boulders, logs) come from copies.
3. **Smaller `d` for Scatters high in the stack.** `d = 3` keeps 99.4% of
   the greedy result in the densest case and saves `2R`.

There are two ways to build the windows:

- **A. Recompute everything in the window.** Each chunk places every
  Scatter above it over the stacked margins, from candidates. No
  dependency on other chunks, but the top of the stack pays the full
  stacked window (about 4.9× the chunk's area for trees in the example).
- **B. Reuse neighbours' finished copies for the masks above.** A
  Scatter's own `K_d` must still be recomputed over its own margin
  (`K_d` over a chunk needs `K_{d−1}` beyond it, which no finished
  neighbour holds). But the masks from the Scatters above come from
  finished copies, and those are exactly what neighbours' cached chunks
  hold. Each Scatter then pays only its own `S + J` margin (about 2× the
  chunk's area for trees), at the cost of placing a Scatter's neighbour
  chunks before a chunk of the Scatter below it.

Both give identical copies. B is cheaper and fits a streaming loader that
places rings of chunks anyway; A is simpler and suits one-off queries. The
spike compares them.

## 5. What the GPU spike must prove

A later spike, on a real GPU (wgpu through Bevy, headless, WGSL compute).
Throwaway code in its own crate outside the workspace members, or behind a
spike flag.

**Inputs**

- A 1 km × 1 km `HeightGrid` (or one Terrain region once it implements
  `Ground`), cut into 64 m chunks.
- One Ecotope stack: trees (ash large + aspen small at scale 4, the
  densest prototype case), boulders (`R = 1.5 m`, `F = 2 m`), logs
  (`R = 1 m`, `F = 3 m`) and grass (200 per m², plant's tile, no spacing).
- An uneven coverage mask, and the same lattice, hashes, ranks and `K_d`
  as the CPU prototype, ported to Rust and WGSL.

**Measurements**

1. **Parity.** For 100 chunks, the set of copy ids, the fixed-point
   positions and the mask texels from the GPU equal the CPU's bit for
   bit, for `d = 1, 3, 5, 7`.
2. **Order and chunk size.** The same 100 chunks built in three orders
   (row, spiral, random) and at chunk sizes 32, 64 and 128 m give the same
   copies.
3. **GPU time per chunk**, split into candidates, density, footing, each
   `K_d` round, stamping, and the mask product, on one discrete GPU and one
   integrated GPU (the user's Mac).
4. **Window overhead**: time with the margins of section 4, built as A
   (recompute the window) and as B (reuse neighbours' finished copies),
   against time with no margin.
5. **CPU twin time** to place one chunk of each Scatter for queries,
   including every Scatter above it over the stacked margins. The budget
   applies to the lowest Scatter that supports per-copy queries (logs in
   this stack), not to trees alone.
6. **Mask memory** per chunk at the resolutions of section 2.
7. **Slot choice** (for D3): the per-copy cost in the placement kernel of
   the integer jump hash and of rendezvous hashing, at `N` = 4, 16 and 64,
   and with a Scatter subset of 4 slots out of 64. Parity with the CPU for
   both.

**Pass or fail**

- Parity (1) and order (2): zero differences. Anything else fails the spike,
  and the cause must be found before C2.
- The whole four-Scatter stack places a 64 m chunk in **≤ 2 ms** of GPU time
  on the integrated GPU, and the tree Scatter's `K_5` in **≤ 0.5 ms**.
  (Proposed budgets, to be confirmed by the user.)
- The CPU twin places one chunk of the lowest queryable Scatter, with
  everything above it, in **≤ 5 ms** on one core, so a query
  or promotion doesn't hitch. If grass fails this, grass answers queries
  from its density only.
- If the window overhead is above 3× with the better of A and B, adopt
  the cap of section 4.

**What D3 needs from it.** WGSL has no 64-bit integers, so the published
jump hash (64-bit LCG, `f64` divide) can't run as is. The prototype
([d2/slots_prototype.py](d2/slots_prototype.py),
[output](d2/slots_prototype.out)) checks an integer-only variant: a 32-bit
hash per step and the jump as `((b + 1) << 16) / ((h >> 16) + 1)`, exact in
`u32` for `N ≤ 2^15`. Over 200,000 keys, its balance (within 4% of even at
`N = 64`) and its moves when `N` grows by one (1.56% against an ideal
1.54%, always to the new slot) match rendezvous hashing. So the choice
comes down to the spike's GPU cost per copy (a jump hash takes about
`ln N` steps, rendezvous `N` hashes) and how subsets are done: rendezvous
scores only the subset's slots; jump hashing picks an index into the
subset's list.

## Spike results (2026-11-23)

The spike is a standalone crate outside the workspace,
[d2/spike/](d2/spike/), with `wgpu` 30.0.2 (the version Bevy resolves) and
no other dependency. It has the integer maths of sections 2–4 in WGSL
([place.wgsl](d2/spike/src/place.wgsl)) and as a CPU twin in Rust
([int.rs](d2/spike/src/int.rs), [cpu.rs](d2/spike/src/cpu.rs)). Run it with

of the final code is checked in as
[d2/spike/results/all.txt](d2/spike/results/all.txt), and every number

**Hardware.** Apple M1 Max (24-core GPU, Metal, reported as an integrated
GPU), with timestamp queries per compute pass. **No discrete GPU was run.**
The M1 Max is also far faster than a typical integrated GPU (an Intel or AMD
laptop iGPU), so its timings flatter the "integrated GPU" budget. The Mac
was shared with other agents' builds and tests.

**The stack.** Trees: ash large + aspen small at scale 4, 0.5 m cells,

### Timings

Timings cover the interior 8 × 8 chunks of the 1 km World, with medians
over the chunks, all from the "Sustained" lines of `all.txt` (plain `K_d`
kernel). "Sustained" puts 16 chunks in one submit, as a running game keeps
the GPU busy. Two encodings were timed:

- **One compute pass per batch**, as an engine would encode it, with
  timestamps only at the pass's ends. This is the number to compare with
  the budget. Wall time per chunk is within 0.1 ms of it.
- **One compute pass per kernel** (about 30 per chunk in window A), with
  timestamps on every pass for the per-stage breakdown. The sum of the
  passes matches the one-pass time. The time from first to last timestamp,
  though, runs about 35% higher (1.58 ms at `d` = 5) and wall time 65%
  higher (1.95 ms): every pass boundary costs time on Metal. **Encode a
  chunk's kernels in one pass.**

With one chunk per submit, the final run matched these numbers. Three
earlier runs of the same code on the same machine were 1.8–2.4× slower: the
M1 Max clocks down when it idles between submits. The cold first chunk
took 3.8 ms of GPU time and 5.4 ms of wall time.

|------------------------------|--------:|--------:|--------:|
| Whole stack, window A, one pass (GPU) | 0.65 | 1.13 | 1.80 |
| Whole stack, window B, one pass (GPU) | 0.49 | 0.74 | 1.07 |
| Whole stack, window A, sum of passes | 0.70 | 1.18 | 1.91 |

Window B per Scatter at `d` = 5: trees 0.38, boulders 0.13, logs 0.12,
grass 0.16 ms. In window A, trees' `K_d` is two thirds of the stack.
Everything else is small: candidates with density and footing 0.008–0.017 ms
per Scatter, mask products about 0.01 ms, stamps 0.02–0.06 ms, grass
candidates 0.08 ms.

`K_d` uses the plain stencil kernel: one thread per cell, early exit on
dead cells, and the ±⌈R/c⌉ neighbours read from global memory. A variant
that loads each 16 × 16 block and its halo into workgroup memory was
bit-identical but 30% slower when sustained. With wgpu's default zeroing of
workgroup memory on, it was 30× slower: turn that off for kernels that
write their workgroup memory before reading it. Section 4's 0.5 ms
assumed stream compaction of the survivors, which the spike did not try.

Windows (`d` = 5, 0.5 m footprint texels): trees 136 m square in window A
(4.5× the chunk's area) and 89 m in window B (1.9×). Boulders 108 / 84 m,
logs 82 / 74 m, grass 64 m. `K_d`'s stencil rounds `R` up to whole cells,
so boulders pay `S` = 5 × 2 cells = 10 m, not 7.5 m.

CPU twin, one core, window A, mean ms per stage when placing down to logs
(`d` = 5): candidates 2.9, `K_d` 4.8, stamps 1.8, mask products 0.7. The
twin is unoptimised: bounds-checked grids, and `K_d` builds an edge list
per call.

### Mask resolution (item 6)

Measured on the CPU over 16 chunks, against exact integer disc tests at the
copies' true footprints. "Depth" is how far inside a footprint a leaked copy
stands. Two trunk footprints were tried. The 1 m of the stack above is
larger than the 0.6 m aspen spacing, so its discs cover most of the ground
on this dense stand, and nearly every candidate sits near an edge. A 0.25 m
trunk, below every spacing, is the fair case, shown here (boulders and logs
keep `F` = 2 m and 3 m):

| Footprint masks (0.25 m trunk) | Boulders and logs inside an upper disc | Depth, mean / max | Grass kept inside a disc | Grass kept vs exact | Bytes per chunk (3 masks, window A) |
|--------------------------------|---------------------------------------:|------------------:|-------------------------:|--------------------:|------------------------------------:|
| None (baseline) | 812 of 3,444 (24%) | 0.52 / 1.95 m | 3.3 million of 4.5 million | +273% | 0 |
| 0.25 m texels | 108 of 2,677 (4.0%) | 0.06 / 0.21 m | 88k of 1.50 million | −0.15% | 364 KB |
| 0.5 m texels | 184 of 2,659 (6.9%) | 0.09 / 0.50 m | 167k of 1.50 million | −0.20% | 92 KB |
| 1 m texels (boulders' and logs' lattice cell) | 271 of 2,675 (10%) | 0.11 / 0.74 m | 273k of 1.48 million | −1.3% | 24 KB |

- **A bilinear read of a 0/255 stamp gives an edge about one texel soft.** A
  candidate within about a texel of a disc's edge, inside or out, sees a
  blend, so it is thinned instead of kept or removed. Leaks reach up to one
  texel deep. The masks remove most of the overlap (24% → 6.9% at 0.5 m)
  and keep the total count right (−0.20%), but the hole is blurred. At
  0.5 m, 11% of the blades that an exact test keeps are thinned out, and
  about as many leak inside. Halving the texel roughly halves the leak
  depth, at 4× the memory. Following boulders' and logs' 1 m lattice cell
  makes it worse, so **footprint masks should not follow a coarse lattice
  cell.** The 1 m trunk case shows the same pattern, with every rate
  higher (21% of boulders and logs inside a disc at 0.5 m).
- **Growing the stamp radius does not fix it.** One texel more (0.5 m)
  removes the lattice leaks (0 of 1,216) and nearly all grass leaks, but it
  also clears 65% of the grass the exact test keeps (82% with the 1 m
  trunk).
- **Influence at 2 m is fine** for a field this smooth (features 13–29 m
  across). The error is at the quantisation step at 1, 2 and 4 m alike. A
  first Ecotope with sharp coverage edges should check again.

### Also confirmed

- The integer `K_d` behaves like the float prototype: on a 128 m square of
  the densest mix, `K_1` keeps 0.769 of greedy, `K_3` 0.997, and `K_5`
  matches greedy exactly. Odd rounds are nested subsets of greedy with no
  spacing violations. Even rounds break spacing (382 violations at `K_2`).
  Survivors at 40% density are a subset of those at 100%.
- CPU and GPU agree with 32-bit PCG hashes, `u24` ranks compared by
  cross-multiplying with the 8-bit local mask, ties broken by cell, 1/256 m
  fixed point, and fixed-point bilinear reads (`(a·(256−w) + b·w)` in
  `u32`). Nothing needed a float.

### What changed in the recommendations (for the user to decide)

No decision above is changed here. Three results bear on them:

1. **`K_5` and the CPU twin meet their budgets only with window B.** On
   the GPU, window B is the cheaper build everywhere (0.74 ms against
   1.13 ms for the stack). The CPU twin meets 5 ms for logs only if it
   keeps the copies of the Scatters above per chunk, instead of placing
   the whole stack again for each query. Recommendation: make window B
   the streaming build. For queries, give the CPU a cache of placed copies
   per chunk and Scatter. That still places on the CPU and reads nothing
   back (decision 5). A query into a chunk the cache doesn't hold costs at
   least window A's 10 ms, and more if whole neighbour chunks must be
   placed first. Trees at `d` = 3 do not rescue window A on the CPU
   (7.3 ms).
2. **Hard footprints should not come only from bilinearly read masks.**
   Decision 7 says hard footprints come from copies. The spike shows that a
   blended mask leaves the edge about one texel soft, so copies still stand
   up to a texel inside a trunk or a boulder. A candidate option, **not
   tried in the spike**: lattice Scatters test exact discs against the kept
   copies above. That is the same integer gather as the stamp kernel, done
   per candidate, and it is exact on both sides. The soft blended masks
   would stay for density and influence. Decision 4 (resolutions) could
   then settle at 2 m for influence, with footprint masks only driving
   grass's density near copies. If a one-texel-soft edge is acceptable
   instead, 0.5 m stays the right footprint resolution: 0.25 m costs 4×
   the memory to halve the depth.
3. **The 0.5 ms `K_5` budget needs either window B or a faster kernel**
   (compaction, untried) on the M1 Max. A typical integrated GPU will be
   several times slower than this one. Before the budget is confirmed, a
   run on a low-end iGPU and a discrete GPU is still owed.

### Shortcuts in the spike

- Masks and flags are `u32` storage buffers, one value per texel or cell,
  not `R8Uint` textures. Stamping gathers kept copies per texel instead of
  scattering with `atomicMin`, which gives the same texels. Positions are
  World fixed point over a 1 km World, not relative to the chunk; the maths
  only uses differences, so this changes nothing.
- No `HeightGrid` or `Ground`: analytic coverage and slope, quantised on the
  CPU. No heights are produced. Boulder and log densities and every
  footprint are guesses. The 1 m trunk footprint, used by the parity,
  order and timing runs, is too large for the 0.6 m aspen spacing, which
  is why the mask study adds a 0.25 m trunk.
- One footprint texel size per run. "Follow the lattice cell" was judged
  from the 1 m run, not from mixed resolutions.
- Slot costs come from a standalone kernel minus a baseline, not from inside
  the placement kernel.
- Whole-area grass is compared by a 64-bit order-independent digest. The
  parity test compares grass slot by slot.
- Only one GPU. The timings come from a shared, loaded machine. The
  sustained numbers were steady across four runs; the isolated ones varied
  about 2×.
- The stage breakdown puts each kernel in its own compute pass, which adds
  per-pass overhead on Metal; the budget verdicts use the one-pass numbers.

## 6. Open questions for the user

1. **`d` for `K_d`.** 5 (recommended: matches today's result at all but 4 of
   62,000 trees in the densest case), 3 (cheaper, about 0.5% fewer trees
   when dense), or 7 (matched exactly in every test)? Should it be one
   constant, or per Scatter?
2. **Accept the local-edit effect.** A local mask change can change
   survivors up to `d × R` outside it. Fine as stated?
3. **Integer placement.** 32-bit hashes, fixed-point positions at
   1/256 m relative to the chunk, and 8-bit masks read without hardware
   filtering. This changes every tree's position from today's (the hash
   changes). Accept? (No compatibility is needed, per the house rules.)
4. **Mask resolutions.** 0.5 m for footprints, 2 m for influence, 8 bits.
   Or tie footprint masks to each Scatter's lattice cell?
5. **No readback for queries.** Queries recompute placement on the CPU.
   OK, with grass falling back to density-only queries if it is too slow?
6. **Plant's tiles.** Keep them as grass's candidate source under the
   shared contract (recommended), or move grass to the lattice too?
7. **Margin cap.** Cap a stack's window at one chunk ring, and have the
   Ecotope reject or warn beyond it? And should canopy shade always come
   from the tree Scatter's density field?
8. **Spike budgets.** Are 2 ms per chunk for the stack (integrated GPU),
   0.5 ms for `K_5`, and 5 ms for the CPU twin the right targets?
